    Picture an entrepreneur.

    Did you picture a 20-year-old in a hoodie who dropped out of college with one genius idea? Most people do. Research says that picture is mostly wrong, and that is good news if you are still choosing a major.

    This site walks through USF's Entrepreneurship & Innovation (E&I) major and makes one argument: entrepreneurs are built, not born. It is a set of skills you can learn, and those skills pay off whether or not you ever start a company.

    What is Entrepreneurship & Innovation?

    E&I is the study of how new value gets created: how people spot a problem, design a solution, test it, and turn it into something that lasts.

    At USF, E&I is a Bachelor of Science in Business Administration (BSBA) in the School of Management. Its curriculum "emphasizes creative and analytical thinking in the development of socially responsible ventures" (USF School of Management, n.d.-b).

    Students learn to identify problems, design products and services, plan a venture's finances and marketing, and pitch their ideas to real investors.

    Central questions the field asks

    • What problem is worth solving, and who has it?
    • How can we test a solution cheaply before betting on it?
    • How do we create value for customers and society, not only profit?
    • How do we turn an idea into an organization that lasts?
    1. ProblemFind a real need shared by many people.
    2. IdeaDesign a solution that is different from what exists.
    3. TestInterview customers, prototype, collect evidence.
    4. LaunchBuild, pitch, and grow the venture.

    Why does it matter today?

    New ventures are how ideas become products, jobs, and solutions to public problems, from health tech to climate tools.

    Case study

    $100K

    Silvaye: wildfire risk from space

    USF students Owen Sordillo and Andrew Saah, an E&I major, co-founded Silvaye, a startup that uses NASA technology, satellite data, and ground sensors to assess wildfire risk. The project earned a $100,000 grant from a NASA challenge.

    (Breen, 2025)

    Silvaye shows what makes the field relevant: entrepreneurs take tools that already exist and point them at a problem that affects whole communities.

    That focus on impact is built into USF's program, which centers on socially responsible ventures (USF School of Management, n.d.-b).

    Professor June Y. Lee adds that AI makes building a product easier than ever, so the skill that matters most is "identifying meaningful problems and creating solutions that people actually want" (J. Y. Lee, personal communication, November 12, 2026).

    The misconception

    MYTH
    "You're either a born entrepreneur or you're not, and you need a totally original, world-changing idea."

    This idea is everywhere: movies about tech founders, pitch shows on TV, legends about college dropouts. If it were true, studying entrepreneurship would be pointless. You either have "it" or you don't.

    Professor Lee hears a version of this from students all the time. "Many students think they need a groundbreaking or completely original idea," she says. "In reality, successful entrepreneurship often involves improving existing products, services, or business models" (J. Y. Lee, personal communication, November 12, 2026).

    So is the myth true? Researchers have tested it. The next page shows what they found.

    Page 5

    What does research reveal?

    Two well-known studies take the "born entrepreneur" myth apart.

    Gartner, 1988

    It's behavior, not personality

    Entrepreneurship scholar William Gartner reviewed decades of studies that tried to find a special "entrepreneur personality." They found no consistent traits that separated entrepreneurs from other business owners. Gartner argued that entrepreneurs should be defined by what they do, which is creating organizations, rather than who they are.

    Why it matters: behaviors can be learned and practiced.

    (Gartner, 1988)

    Azoulay et al., 2020

    Experience beats youth

    45average founder age

    Using U.S. Census Bureau data, these economists found that the average founder of the fastest-growing 1-in-1,000 new U.S. companies was 45 years old. Prior experience in the same industry strongly predicted success.

    (Azoulay et al., 2020)

    What this means for you: entrepreneurship is a skill set you build over time through practice and experience. That is exactly what a college major is designed to do.

    Inside the discourse community: the pitch

    Every field has its own way of sharing knowledge. For entrepreneurs, the signature genre is the pitch.

    In a few minutes, a founder has to define a problem, show evidence, explain a business model, and ask for support. Investors and judges respond with questions that test the evidence. This back-and-forth is how the community decides which ideas deserve resources.

    Our artifact: USF News's article on the 2026 Innovation Showcase. More than 50 students from across campus submitted ideas, 50 industry professionals reviewed them, and six finalists pitched to a panel of entrepreneurs and investors. Winners received grants of $1,000 to $4,000 (Elliot, 2026).

    The article shows the community's values. Each pitch is summarized by the problem it solves and who it helps, ideas are sorted into "Idea" and "Impact" stages, and success is measured in funding. Professor Lee describes the same standard: a strong pitch shows "evidence, not just enthusiasm" (J. Y. Lee, personal communication, November 12, 2026).

    Anatomy of a pitch deck

    1. ProblemWho hurts, and how much?
    2. SolutionWhat you built
    3. MarketHow many people need it
    4. Business modelHow it makes money
    5. TeamWhy you can do it
    6. AskWhat you need

    Hi, I'm Mike Chezan, a first-year business student at USF.

    In an email interview, Professor June Lee told us, "You don't have to be born an entrepreneur. You develop entrepreneurial thinking by practicing it." That stood out to me, because I grew up in a family of entrepreneurs.

    Watching them, I learned that success has less to do with where you start. Anyone can begin as an entrepreneur. In my experience, the ones who succeed are the ones who stay consistent through the process and remain committed to their idea, even when it gets difficult.

    The research supports this too. A 2020 study by economist Pierre Azoulay and his colleagues found that the average founder of the fastest-growing new companies in the U.S. was 45 years old (Azoulay et al., 2020). So you don't need to have everything figured out at eighteen.

    Professor Lee makes a similar point: execution and adaptability matter just as much as the idea itself.

    So if you're undecided about your major, don't wait until you feel like a "natural." Start practicing, and stay with it.

    Read highlights from the full interview

    What do students misunderstand about entrepreneurship?"Entrepreneurship is not just about starting a company. It is about identifying problems, recognizing opportunities, and creating value through innovative solutions."

    Can entrepreneurial skills be learned?"Entrepreneurship is a mindset and a set of skills that can be developed through education and experience." It is "best learned through hands-on experiences: developing ideas, interviewing potential customers, testing assumptions, and learning from failures."

    Students from any major can join the E&I incubator, enter pitch competitions, get mentoring from professional entrepreneurs, and apply for microgrants. One student used a microgrant to build a medical-device prototype that led to $100,000 in angel funding.

    A new perspective

    We are both business students, and we expected this project to confirm what we already knew. Instead, we learned much more about what it takes to succeed as a business graduate, in entrepreneurship and in every other field.

    What we thought

    • Entrepreneurs are born with a special personality.
    • You have to start young to succeed.
    • You need one genius, never-seen-before idea.
    • The major only matters if you plan to found a startup.

    What we found

    • Entrepreneurship is a learnable set of behaviors (Gartner, 1988).
    • Experience predicts success, and top founders average 45 (Azoulay et al., 2020).
    • Improving existing products counts (J. Y. Lee, personal communication, November 12, 2026).
    • The skills work inside any company, nonprofit, or family business.

    Our biggest surprise

    45average age of the most successful founders

    The fact that surprised us most was the average age of founders behind the fastest-growing new U.S. companies: 45 (Azoulay et al., 2020). We had assumed success belonged to people who figured everything out by their early twenties. Learning that experience matters more than youth changed how we see our own futures: there is no deadline for having it all figured out.

    Why consider this major?

    Reason one

    You can learn it

    Entrepreneurship is defined by behavior, not inborn traits, so a major can teach it (Gartner, 1988).

    Reason two

    You practice for real

    Pitch competitions, an incubator, and microgrants let you test ideas with real investors while still a student (Breen, 2025; Elliot, 2026).

    Reason three

    It pays off anywhere

    As AI handles routine work, creativity and problem-solving matter more in every career (J. Y. Lee, personal communication, November 12, 2026).

    Entrepreneurs are built, not born. USF is a place to build.
